Scientific Overview
Can LLMs predict how people respond — not just what they say?
SimBench is the first large-scale benchmark for group-level human behavior simulation. The task isn't to answer a survey question correctly — it's to predict the full distribution of how a demographic group responds. As of 2026, the best model closes only 40% of the gap to ground truth. But the failures follow a clear pattern, and each pattern is something we can fix.

Scrye uses SimBench as its evaluation target and draws on the literature to make principled bets about what interventions will move the score — and why.

How the score works: SimBench score S = 100 · (1 − TVD(P,Q) / TVD(P,U)) — where P is the human distribution, Q is the model's prediction, and U is uniform random. S = 100 means perfect; S = 0 means no better than guessing uniformly; S < 0 means the model is actively miscalibrated. The key insight: this is a distributional metric — it rewards reproducing the full spread of human responses, not just picking the modal answer.

Three Structural Failure Modes — and What They Mean for Us

SimBench's headline number (40.8/100) would be discouraging if the errors were random. They aren't. The same failure patterns appear across models, datasets, and research groups — which means they're diagnosable, and potentially fixable.

⚖️
The Alignment–Simulation Tradeoff r = −0.942
The same training that makes a model helpful makes it a worse simulator. RLHF teaches models to minimize mode-seeking KL — collapsing outputs toward the "best" single answer. Pre-training minimizes mass-covering KL — covering all plausible human responses.

The effect: on consensus questions (low entropy), alignment helps up to +40 S-points. But past response entropy ≈ 0.8, an aligned model is a worse simulator than its base model. The same process that creates a polite, helpful assistant systematically destroys the diversity of outputs needed to match real human heterogeneity.
→ Lever: base/merged models for high-entropy items
🚫
Demographic Conditioning Backfires
Adding a persona prompt — "You are from Finland, age 30–49, female" — should help the model produce that group's responses. Instead, it consistently hurts. On SimBenchGrouped, every tested demographic category shows negative ΔS: religiosity/practice −9.91, political ideology −4.97, religion −4.83, gender −1.24.

Asking the model to "be" a group makes it caricature that group, collapsing within-group diversity toward a stereotype. The finding is robust across all 45 models tested.
→ Lever: delta-prediction, not persona embodiment
🧠
Reasoning Doesn't Help — Knowledge Does
Simulation accuracy correlates strongly with world knowledge (MMLU-Pro r = 0.94, GPQA r = 0.86) but barely with reasoning (AIME r = 0.48) or conversational skill (Chatbot Arena r = 0.71).

More tellingly, chain-of-thought hurts: o4-mini with more inference compute goes 27.77 → 28.99; CoT degrades GPT-4.1 from 34.55 → 33.11. Human survey responses are heuristic and intuitive — forcing deliberate reasoning on them is exactly wrong.
→ Implication: cache-and-route, don't deliberate
📈
Fine-Tuning on Behavior Closes the Gap
The constructive finding: Beyond Believability (arXiv:2503.20749) shows that fine-tuning an LLM on real behavioral traces improves action-prediction accuracy by ~21–25% over prompt-only agents at matched scale.

This is the empirical kernel of Scrye's approach. Distribution-preserving post-training (Centaur, Binz et al. 2025) works better than general instruction tuning — evidence that the alignment–simulation tradeoff is not a fundamental barrier, but a training objective choice.
→ Bet: telemetry-grounded prediction beats prompting
Six Testable Hypotheses

Each finding above points to a specific, falsifiable claim we can test against SimBench. These are ordered by leverage — from the core load-bearing test down to calibration details. Click any to expand.

H1
The value–action gap holds for content behavior
Load-bearing ▶
Content-engagement behavior (finish, re-watch, churn, pay) is predicted markedly worse than stated preferences — with the gap resembling SimBench's attitude→behavior drop (~0.83 → ~0.66).

Test: Pair stated-preference prediction vs. revealed-behavior prediction on the same titles; measure both in normalized, distributional terms. If the gap is large → validates the tiered-confidence architecture. If small → the defensible surface is bigger than the literature implies (upside surprise).
H2
Telemetry-grounded fine-tuning closes the behavior gap
Core bet ▶
A model fine-tuned on behavioral traces beats prompt-only generative agents on held-out action prediction by ~21–25%, consistent with the Beyond Believability result.

Test: Prompt-only agent vs. telemetry-tuned model on held-out completion/churn. Evidence base: arXiv:2503.20749; Centaur (Binz et al. 2025).
H3
Distribution-preserving models outperform aligned ones on diverse items
Moat candidate ▶
On high-entropy / heterogeneous slices, base or model-merged models outperform aligned frontier models — the alignment–simulation tradeoff (r=−0.942) appears in content data too.

Test: Base vs. instruct vs. merged on high- vs. low-entropy audience segments. Evidence: SimBench r=−0.942; Hu/Minixhofer/Collier model-merging (arXiv:2510.17426).
H4
Persona vectors detect miscalibration — they don't cause accuracy
Governance ▶
A "stereotype-direction" projection (representation engineering) predicts when an agent is miscalibrated (collapsed toward a demographic prior) better than persona conditioning improves accuracy.

Test: Regress prediction error on the magnitude of activation projection onto extracted stereotype directions. Compare against accuracy delta from naive persona conditioning. Evidence: Hu & Collier 2024; Anthropic Persona Vectors (arXiv:2507.21509).
H5
Rank-order is shippable; magnitude is not
Product ▶
Simulated rankings of content correlate well with realized rankings (high rank-order r) while absolute magnitude predictions are systematically inflated and poorly calibrated.

Why it matters: If confirmed, the product spec writes itself — calibrated rank + explicit confidence tier + refused point-magnitude. Park finds sign r=0.91–0.99 but broken magnitude; Bisbee et al. find 7× polarization inflation. The literature strongly predicts this.
H6
Cold-start extrapolation has signal, but can't be magnitude-calibrated
Cold start ▶
For novel content with no behavioral history, placing the item semantically against telemetry-anchored neighbors beats chance — but can't be magnitude-calibrated without labels.

Test: Hold out whole content clusters; measure rank recovery from semantic placement alone. Sets the honest ceiling for the cold-start tier. Evidence: SimBench MMLU-Pro r=0.94 correlation establishes the semantic-extrapolation floor.

Directions Considered But Not Adopted

An honest overview names the roads not taken. Several promising ideas were pursued in depth and rejected, one was fully specified and deferred for time, and one remains the open frontier.

Settled
Model choice is the dominant lever — not a commodity. Swapping Gemini-2.5 → 3.1-flash-lite moved the faithful baseline +15.9 (19.3 → 35.2); the whole method stack adds +5.5 on top. Above a capability floor frontier models cluster, but the margin is decisive — and a 22×-pricier flagship was not better.
Rejected
Bottom-up persona simulation, even census-grounded. Stage 18 replaced generic synthetic personas with Nemotron-Personas-USA (a million census-grounded US adults). Over five rounds it solved the spread problem (predicted entropy 0.644 vs. truth 0.692 — the first persona simulator in the project to get the shape right) and supplied a missing ideology axis (→ 51.8), yet stayed ~11 points below the direct calibrated_commitment ask (63.2) and added nothing in ensemble (+0.5). The binding constraint is location, not spread — the fourth confirmation (Stages 03 / 07 / 13 / 18) that elaborate bottom-up methods lose to the direct distributional ask. Persona line closed.
Rejected
Elaborate reasoning & post-hoc calibration. Superforecaster and diversity-CoT prompting, Monte-Carlo individuals, and temperature / entropy / Dirichlet calibration all lose to the plain distributional ask. Reasoning-first is a tax on this task, and spread cannot be fixed without first fixing the mode.
Deferred
Agentic web retrieval. The benchmark brief permits internet tools, so we fully preregistered a WebAgentPredictor — a bounded tool-calling agent over guarded, disk-cached web search/fetch, with identity-stripping + a domain blocklist (so it cannot look up the survey's published results) and a closed-book twin as a control. Held, not run — deferred for time, not for lack of promise. The most natural next lever.
Open frontier
Does accumulated behavioral data predict staked behavior? The bottom-right empty cell of the evidence matrix — untested by anyone. It is Scrye's core Part II bet and its largest risk, and the reason for the Part II feedback-architecture design (docs/presentation/part-ii-feedback-architecture.md).
Experiment Design
How do you iterate on ideas without accidentally cheating?
Scrye explores many possible improvements — different prompting strategies, model choices, calibration methods — before committing to a final answer. The challenge: every design decision is implicitly "fit to data," even without gradient training. If you evaluate on the same data you're using to make decisions, you'll fool yourself into thinking ideas work that don't.

The solution is a three-part discipline: a principled data split that prevents leakage, a pre-registered set of interventions that freezes the search space before any test-set contact, and a statistical guardrail (the Ladder gate) that controls how much information the validation set reveals per query — bounding the overfitting that would otherwise creep in through repeated evaluation.
The Data: SimBench Records

Each record is a typed SimBenchRecord: a survey question, the answer options, a demographic segment (country × attribute), and the empirical response distribution from real respondents. SimBench ships two evaluation splits — population (unconditioned) and grouped (demographic-conditioned) — but no train/dev partition. We construct the holdout structure entirely.

SimBenchPop — population split
7,167 cases across 20 datasets. Each case uses the benchmark's default group prompt — no demographic conditioning. This is the broader coverage split.
SimBenchGrouped — demographic split
6,343 cases across 5 surveys (ESS, Afrobarometer, LatinoBarometro, OpinionQA, ISSP). Each case is conditioned on a specific country × demographic attribute pair, e.g. {"cntry": "Finland", "age_group": "30-49"}.
The Three-Way Split

We partition records into three non-overlapping buckets — but the unit of assignment is a question family, not a row. SimBench's grouped split has many near-duplicate variants of each survey item (one per demographic segment). A random row split would scatter siblings across buckets, effectively evaluating the model on questions it was tuned on. We prevent this by keeping all variants of a question together.

dev50%
val25%
test25%
dev — iterate freely
Error analysis, comparing simulation systems, ablating design choices. The experiments notebook runs exclusively here. No restrictions.
val — gated access
Model selection and calibration fitting. Accessed only when the Ladder gate admits a candidate. Each query costs one unit of the validation budget K.
test — locked
Scored exactly once, at the very end, for the final report. The three required assignment questions are pinned here and never inform a design decision.
How assignment works: Each question family is hashed (seeded SHA-256) to a value in [0, 1) and placed by fixed cumulative-fraction thresholds. Assignment depends only on the family key and seed — never on the rest of the dataset — so the partition is stable under additions or removals. The three required assessment questions are extracted first and pinned to test before fractions are applied.
The Lever Registry — a Pre-Committed Search Space

Before any validation contact, we register a frozen set of theory-motivated interventions — "levers." This pre-commitment is what makes the search statistically honest: by declaring all possible interventions upfront, we bound the multiple-comparisons surface. The loop may only explore these. Proposing anything off-registry is a gated, human-approved act. Each lever carries its scientific hypothesis, mechanism, and expected direction — so rationales are grounded in the SimBench findings, not post-hoc.

Lever ID Hypothesis Scientific Mechanism Expected Direction
recalib.global_temp A single temperature parameter corrects mode-seeking over-confidence. RLHF → mode-seeking KL → over-sharp predicted distributions. Temperature T > 1 flattens them back toward the true spread. ↑ S overall, especially mid-entropy items.
recalib.entropy_temp Apply more correction where the model is already most diffuse. SimBench r=−0.942: the alignment harm is largest on high-entropy items. An entropy-adaptive temperature targets the worst-affected regime without hurting consensus items. ↑ S on diverse items, neutral on consensus.
recalib.dirichlet Additive smoothing restores probability mass to suppressed options. Mode collapse zeros out plausible minority responses. Dirichlet smoothing (q_i ∝ q_i + α) restores tail probability, matching the observed multi-modal human distribution. ↑ S on multi-modal items with under-predicted tails.
model.swap A base or less-aligned model is a better simulator on high-entropy items. Mass-covering pretraining > mode-seeking RLHF when the target is diverse human responses. For items past entropy ≈ 0.8, alignment actively hurts. ↑ S on high-entropy items; may reduce need for recalibration.
predictor.swap Predict the distribution shift from population, rather than "be this group." SimBench: all demographic conditioning strategies degrade accuracy. Predicting the delta avoids the caricature collapse while still incorporating conditioning information. ↑ counterfactual alignment without persona degradation.
The Search Loop — How We Explore Safely

A proposer selects a lever, applies it to the current pipeline configuration, and evaluates the result on dev — freely, as many times as needed. Dev improvement triggers a validation query: a single evaluation on the held-out val set. Every val query goes through the Ladder gate (explained below), which decides whether the improvement is real or noise. The entire process is logged to a DAG ledger, enabling resumability and auditability.

The Ladder Gate — Preventing Noise Overfitting

Every time we query the validation set to check whether an improvement is real, we spend a little of our statistical budget — the more we query, the more likely we are to accidentally find a result that looks good on val but won't generalize. The Ladder gate (Blum & Hardt 2015) solves this by raising the bar each time we query.

How the gate works
A candidate is promoted only if its val score exceeds the running best by more than a threshold η:

accept iff score > best + η

Under this rule, the held-out estimate's generalization error is bounded by ~(log(k·n)/n)^(1/3) regardless of how many candidates k are tried. An adaptive search cannot climb a noise ladder.
How η is calibrated
η is not guessed — it's estimated from bootstrap resampling of a reference pipeline's val scores:

η = mult × std(bootstrap_means)

This gives the noise scale of the val mean-S: the smallest difference between two pipelines that's distinguishable from finite-sample wobble. Computed once, before any val queries. mult ≥ 1.0 keeps the gate conservative.
Measurement

Two complementary metrics give a full picture — how accurate the distribution is in absolute terms, and whether conditioning on demographics moves the prediction in the right direction.

📊 SimBench Score S — primary
S = 100·(1 − TVD(P,Q)/TVD(P,U))

Measured as mean S across records + 95% percentile bootstrap CI (2,000 resamples). Normalizers are built from the full split (not the eval subsample) so scores are comparable across systems. Reported with an entropy-binned breakdown to diagnose the alignment–simulation tradeoff per regime.
🧭 Counterfactual Sensitivity — secondary
Measures whether demographic conditioning moves predictions in the right direction, even when absolute magnitude is off. A system can have poor absolute S but correct directional sensitivity — useful for diagnosing whether predictor strategies are doing anything structurally useful vs. just adding noise.
